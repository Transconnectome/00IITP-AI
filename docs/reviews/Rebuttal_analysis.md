## Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author):
This study aims to develop and validate a deep learning framework that integrates polygenic risk scores (PGS) with diffusion MRI-derived white matter features to predict the onset and developmental trajectory of depression. The authors utilized a large multi-ethnic youth cohort from the ABCD study (N=4,741) and an independent Korean cohort (N=108), applying PGS to pretrain the model and subsequently fine-tuning it on white matter imaging data for depression classification and prediction. Additionally, the model identified key white matter tracts associated with depression. The study demonstrates certain strengths in methodological design, sample size, cross-population validation, and biological interpretability. However, the methodological descriptions lack sufficient detail, and several analytical approaches, result interpretations, and writing aspects need improvement to enhance the rigor and clarity of the paper. Moreover, given the existence of numerous high-performing multimodal fusion approaches, the lack of comparison with such methods reduces the methodological novelty of this study. In conclusion, the current version lacks critical implementation details, raising concerns about the reliability and reproducibility of the reported findings.
1. Major Strengths
(1) The innovative use of PGS (including both depression PGS and composite PGS) for pretraining the white matter imaging model leverages potential gene-brain structural associations to improve downstream depression prediction; (2) Identification of depression-associated white matter tracts, contributing to biological interpretability; (3) Use of a large ABCD dataset and validation on an independent Korean cohort, as well as longitudinal evaluation of predictive performance; (4) Comprehensive experimental design, but insufficient detail in implementation limits reproducibility.
2. Major Concerns
The methodology and experimental details require substantial clarification and supplementation. While the authors conducted multiple experiments to demonstrate the method's effectiveness from different angles, it is strongly recommended that each experiment be briefly introduced with its purpose and procedure before presenting results, to help readers better follow the research flow. To further enhance clarity, a comprehensive schematic diagram of the entire pipeline (including data partitioning, model training, validation, and evaluation) is strongly encouraged, rather than limiting to the analytical process shown in Figure 1. Importantly, without sufficient methodological transparency, the credibility of the findings is significantly compromised.

We appreciate the reviewer's suggestion to compare against established multimodal fusion approaches. We deliberately chose our simpler, concatenation-based architecture for two principled reasons that directly align with our core research question and the constraints of our specialized dataset.
1. Sample size constraints in pediatric neuroimaging: Our small size of fine-tuning dataset (N=266) reflects a common challenge in pediatric psychiatric neuroimaging research. Sample sizes in this field typically range from 15-200 per diagnostic group [1]. Modern multimodal fusion methods often employ attention-based mechanisms that are widely recognized as being 'data-hungry' and require substantial training data for adequate performance. When trained from small-mid size datasets, Vision Transformer (ViT), a typical model which is based on attention mechanisms, underperform CNNs even in unimodal approach [2]. Furthermore, training deep architectures with complex fusion mechanisms such as cross-modal attention has been shown to be computationally intensive and prone to overfitting when applied to small multimodal datasets [3,4].
Therefore, we adopted a parameter-efficient approach: (1) a relatively efficient 3DCNN backbone, and (2) concatenation-based fusion, which follows the ‘joint fusion’ strategy described by Huang et al. (2020). This approach offers key advantages: it models interactions between multimodal features, learns compatible representations from each modality, and avoids the need to train multiple separate models, all while maintaining parameter efficiency suitable for smaller datasets [5].
2. Research question specificity: Our primary research question was not to optimize multimodal fusion per se, but rather to address the challenge of youth depression prediction in small samples by leveraging prior knowledge about gene-brain associations. This consideration was critical because when the domain gap between pre-training and target datasets is substantial, even sophisticated fusion architectures may not overcome the fundamental challenge of limited target-domain data [6]. Our approach specifically tested whether pretraining on the well-established association between genetic risk and white matter integrity could improve depression prediction in limited data regimes.
This required a targeted comparison: pretrained vs. from-scratch models with identical architectures, isolating the effect of PGS-guided pretraining. Introducing more complex fusion methods (e.g., cross-modal attention) would confound interpretation, performance differences could arise from either the pretraining strategy or the fusion mechanism, obscuring our core research question.
We agree that systematic comparison with advanced fusion architectures represents an important future direction. We have added this to the Discussion (lines 536-540) [Updated Main Text], emphasizing that such approaches may provide additional benefits as larger pediatric depression datasets with multimodal data become available.
Fourth, our study employed a 'joint fusion' strategy rather than more complex, state-of-the-art fusion models. This was a deliberate methodological choice, prioritizing a parameter-efficient model suitable for our modest sample size of finetune set (N=266). However, we acknowledge that this represents a trade-off, and future work with larger, dedicated datasets should explore whether advanced fusion mechanisms could yield further improvements in predictive accuracy.

Decision Curve Analysis
To assess the clinical utility of our model, we conducted Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) by computing net benefit across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities [59]. Net benefit quantifies the balance between true positives and false positives, incorporating the relative harm of unnecessary interventions. It is calculated as:
Net Benefit=(True Positives - False Positives × p_t/(1-p_t )  )/N
where 𝑝 is the threshold probability, and 𝑁 is the sample size. The threshold probability is defined as the minimum probability of disease at which a clinician would opt for an intervention. Mathematically, this threshold serves as the relative weight between the benefit of true positives and the harm of false positives (unnecessary interventions). We compared our best-performing models against two baseline strategies: ‘Treat All’ (assuming all individuals are at risk and should receive intervention) and ‘Treat None’ (assuming no individuals require intervention). Given the balanced nature of our dataset (approximate prevalence of 50%), we evaluated net benefit across threshold probabilities with emphasis on the 0.40–0.60 range where model discrimination is particularly informative given the balanced prevalence. A higher net benefit across threshold probabilities indicates superior clinical applicability by improving the identification of at-risk individuals while minimizing unnecessary interventions.

1) Detailed PGS calculation Methodology
All PGS used in this study were calculated following the methodology established and validated in Joo et al., (2024) [1]. We have added comprehensive section to our Updated Supplementary Materials (Genetic Data Preprocessing Section) as below:
Genotype Quality Control and Preprocessing
The ABCD study collected saliva samples from participants, which were genotyped at Rutgers University Cell and DNA Repository using the Affymetrix SmokeScreen Array consisting of 733,293 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). We applied standard quality control filters using PLINK version 1.9, removing variants with genotype call rates below 95%, samples with call rates below 95%, and variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 1%. Following these initial filters, genotypes were imputed using the Michigan Imputation Server with the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference panel, yielding a total of 12,046,090 SNPs. The imputed data were phased using Eagle v2.4. After imputation, we applied additional stringent quality control measures, removing SNPs with imputation quality scores (INFO) below 0.4, genotype call rates below 95%, Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium p-values less than 1×10⁻²⁰ (appropriate for diverse ethnic populations), sample missingness greater than 5%, MAF below 0.5%, and variants showing extreme heterozygosity (defined as exceeding three standard deviations from the population mean). These comprehensive filters resulted in a final set of 11,221,810 high-quality variants for PGS construction.
Addressing Population Structure and Genetic Relatedness
Given the multi-ethnic composition of the ABCD cohort, we implemented rigorous procedures to address potential population stratification due to genetic relatedness and ancestry admixture. We employed the PC-Air algorithm implemented in the SNPRelate R package to identify genetically unrelated individuals and compute ancestrally informative principal components robust to familial or cryptic relatedness. This process involved two rounds of principal component analysis. In the first round, we obtained initial pairwise kinship coefficient estimates using the KING-robust algorithm on a pruned set of independent genetic variants (linkage disequilibrium threshold r²<0.1). We identified genetically related individuals with kinship coefficients indicating closer than third-degree relatives (kinship threshold = 2⁻⁹/², approximately equivalent to first cousins), retaining only one individual per related pair for the main analysis while setting aside excluded individuals as an independent validation set (n=1,814). The PC-Air algorithm identified an unrelated set of 8,845 individuals based on these pairwise kinship estimates and systematically computed principal components of their genotype data. Subsequently, PC-Relate was applied to compute refined kinship estimates adjusting for ancestry, providing estimates robust to population structure and admixture. The second round of PC-Air then computed accurate principal components using this refined unrelated set. We further excluded 88 participants whose projected principal components had Mahalanobis distances greater than 6 standard deviations from the population mean. This comprehensive process resulted in a final analytic sample of 8,620 unrelated individuals. The 1,814 individuals initially excluded from relatedness analysis were further assessed for third-degree relatedness, yielding a final independent validation set of 1,579 unrelated individuals used for PGS hyperparameter optimization.
PGS Construction Using PRS-CSx
To estimate individual-specific polygenic liability for complex traits, we employed PRS-CSx, a state-of-the-art Bayesian polygenic modeling technique designed for cross-population prediction. PRS-CSx implements an effective posterior inference algorithm that explicitly accounts for population-specific allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns by adopting a shared continuous shrinkage prior across ancestries. This approach is particularly appropriate for multi-ethnic cohorts as it leverages information from multiple ancestry groups to improve prediction accuracy. For PGS construction, we utilized LD reference panels for European-ancestry (EUR) and Admixed Americans (AMR) from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3, selected in accordance with the discovery GWAS samples. Although PRS-CSx can automatically estimate the global shrinkage hyperparameter (φ), we conducted a small-scale grid search across φ values of 1×10⁻⁶, 1×10⁻⁴, 1×10⁻², and 1 for traits where corresponding phenotypes were available in the ABCD dataset, selecting the φ value that maximized explained variance to optimize predictive performance.
Multi-Ancestry PGS Integration
For five complex traits—BMI, PTSD, depression, schizophrenia, and alcohol dependence—GWAS summary statistics were available from both European-ancestry and non-European-ancestry populations. For these traits, we constructed multi-ethnic PGS by combining ancestry-specific GWAS summary statistics through learning the optimal linear combination of ancestry-specific PGS. This approach explicitly models the contribution of each ancestry-specific genetic architecture to trait variation in our admixed population. For example, in constructing multi-ancestry depression PGS, if the optimal φ value for the European-ancestry-based PGS was 1 and for the Admixed-American-based PGS was 1×10⁻², the regression model would be: depression outcome = (GPS_depression_EUR × β₁) + (GPS_depression_AMR × β₂) + (sex × β₃) + (top 10 genetic PCs × β₄...₁₃) + (genetic ancestry × β₁₄) + ε. The β coefficients representing the optimal weighting of each ancestry-specific PGS were estimated through this regression framework to maximize predictive accuracy in our multi-ethnic sample.
PGS Validation and Optimization
After constructing the initial PGS, we validated and optimized them using available phenotypes in the ABCD dataset. For traits with corresponding measures in ABCD, we tuned the global shrinkage hyperparameter by examining the effect size (β coefficient) and significance of the PGS in linear regression models across the grid-searched φ values, selecting the φ that maximized both effect size and model R². This optimization was conducted in the independent validation set of 1,579 unrelated participants who were excluded during the relatedness quality control process, ensuring unbiased hyperparameter selection. Final optimized PGS were then assessed for validity in the full analytic sample of 8,620 participants, with regression models adjusting for sex, age, study site, and genetic ancestry. For 15 GWAS traits that lacked corresponding target phenotypes in the ABCD study, we employed PRS-CS-auto, which automatically selects the hyperparameter φ from data using a fully Bayesian approach, providing pseudo-validation for these traits.

2) Composite PGS construction
Among the 29 traits in Joo et al. (2024) [1], only five PGS, BMI, PTSD, depression, schizophrenia, and alcohol dependence, were constructed as multi-ethnic PGS using both European-ancestry and non-European-ancestry GWAS summary statistics. We selected these five as candidate predictors for our composite PGS because they were the only traits with multi-ancestry GWAS data available, ensuring applicability across the diverse ethnic composition of our ABCD cohort and planned Korean validation sample as it was mentioned above.
From these five candidates, we employed Elastic Net regularization to identify the optimal weighted combination. The target outcome for optimization was a depressive symptom score derived from KSADS-COMP in the pretraining set (n=4,741). We used symptom scores rather than diagnostic status because the pretraining set deliberately excluded all participants with lifetime MDD diagnosis, this exclusion was necessary to maximize the sample size available for the subsequent fine-tuning set where MDD classification was performed. The Elastic Net model was fitted to maximize explained variance (R²) while minimizing the number of independent variables through stepwise elimination across 5,000 resampled train-validation-test splits. 1) Detailed PGS calculation Methodology
All PGS used in this study were calculated following the methodology established and validated in Joo et al., (2024) [1]. We have added comprehensive section to our Updated Supplementary Materials (Genetic Data Preprocessing Section) as below:
Genotype Quality Control and Preprocessing
The ABCD study collected saliva samples from participants, which were genotyped at Rutgers University Cell and DNA Repository using the Affymetrix SmokeScreen Array consisting of 733,293 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). We applied standard quality control filters using PLINK version 1.9, removing variants with genotype call rates below 95%, samples with call rates below 95%, and variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 1%. Following these initial filters, genotypes were imputed using the Michigan Imputation Server with the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference panel, yielding a total of 12,046,090 SNPs. The imputed data were phased using Eagle v2.4. After imputation, we applied additional stringent quality control measures, removing SNPs with imputation quality scores (INFO) below 0.4, genotype call rates below 95%, Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium p-values less than 1×10⁻²⁰ (appropriate for diverse ethnic populations), sample missingness greater than 5%, MAF below 0.5%, and variants showing extreme heterozygosity (defined as exceeding three standard deviations from the population mean). These comprehensive filters resulted in a final set of 11,221,810 high-quality variants for PGS construction.
Addressing Population Structure and Genetic Relatedness
Given the multi-ethnic composition of the ABCD cohort, we implemented rigorous procedures to address potential population stratification due to genetic relatedness and ancestry admixture. We employed the PC-Air algorithm implemented in the SNPRelate R package to identify genetically unrelated individuals and compute ancestrally informative principal components robust to familial or cryptic relatedness. This process involved two rounds of principal component analysis. In the first round, we obtained initial pairwise kinship coefficient estimates using the KING-robust algorithm on a pruned set of independent genetic variants (linkage disequilibrium threshold r²<0.1). We identified genetically related individuals with kinship coefficients indicating closer than third-degree relatives (kinship threshold = 2⁻⁹/², approximately equivalent to first cousins), retaining only one individual per related pair for the main analysis while setting aside excluded individuals as an independent validation set (n=1,814). The PC-Air algorithm identified an unrelated set of 8,845 individuals based on these pairwise kinship estimates and systematically computed principal components of their genotype data. Subsequently, PC-Relate was applied to compute refined kinship estimates adjusting for ancestry, providing estimates robust to population structure and admixture. The second round of PC-Air then computed accurate principal components using this refined unrelated set. We further excluded 88 participants whose projected principal components had Mahalanobis distances greater than 6 standard deviations from the population mean. This comprehensive process resulted in a final analytic sample of 8,620 unrelated individuals. The 1,814 individuals initially excluded from relatedness analysis were further assessed for third-degree relatedness, yielding a final independent validation set of 1,579 unrelated individuals used for PGS hyperparameter optimization.
PGS Construction Using PRS-CSx
To estimate individual-specific polygenic liability for complex traits, we employed PRS-CSx, a state-of-the-art Bayesian polygenic modeling technique designed for cross-population prediction. PRS-CSx implements an effective posterior inference algorithm that explicitly accounts for population-specific allele frequencies and linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns by adopting a shared continuous shrinkage prior across ancestries. This approach is particularly appropriate for multi-ethnic cohorts as it leverages information from multiple ancestry groups to improve prediction accuracy. For PGS construction, we utilized LD reference panels for European-ancestry (EUR) and Admixed Americans (AMR) from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3, selected in accordance with the discovery GWAS samples. Although PRS-CSx can automatically estimate the global shrinkage hyperparameter (φ), we conducted a small-scale grid search across φ values of 1×10⁻⁶, 1×10⁻⁴, 1×10⁻², and 1 for traits where corresponding phenotypes were available in the ABCD dataset, selecting the φ value that maximized explained variance to optimize predictive performance.
Multi-Ancestry PGS Integration
For five complex traits—BMI, PTSD, depression, schizophrenia, and alcohol dependence—GWAS summary statistics were available from both European-ancestry and non-European-ancestry populations. For these traits, we constructed multi-ethnic PGS by combining ancestry-specific GWAS summary statistics through learning the optimal linear combination of ancestry-specific PGS. This approach explicitly models the contribution of each ancestry-specific genetic architecture to trait variation in our admixed population. For example, in constructing multi-ancestry depression PGS, if the optimal φ value for the European-ancestry-based PGS was 1 and for the Admixed-American-based PGS was 1×10⁻², the regression model would be: depression outcome = (GPS_depression_EUR × β₁) + (GPS_depression_AMR × β₂) + (sex × β₃) + (top 10 genetic PCs × β₄...₁₃) + (genetic ancestry × β₁₄) + ε. The β coefficients representing the optimal weighting of each ancestry-specific PGS were estimated through this regression framework to maximize predictive accuracy in our multi-ethnic sample.
PGS Validation and Optimization
After constructing the initial PGS, we validated and optimized them using available phenotypes in the ABCD dataset. For traits with corresponding measures in ABCD, we tuned the global shrinkage hyperparameter by examining the effect size (β coefficient) and significance of the PGS in linear regression models across the grid-searched φ values, selecting the φ that maximized both effect size and model R². This optimization was conducted in the independent validation set of 1,579 unrelated participants who were excluded during the relatedness quality control process, ensuring unbiased hyperparameter selection. Final optimized PGS were then assessed for validity in the full analytic sample of 8,620 participants, with regression models adjusting for sex, age, study site, and genetic ancestry. For 15 GWAS traits that lacked corresponding target phenotypes in the ABCD study, we employed PRS-CS-auto, which automatically selects the hyperparameter φ from data using a fully Bayesian approach, providing pseudo-validation for these traits.

3) Composite PGS construction
Among the 29 traits in Joo et al. (2024) [1], only five PGS, BMI, PTSD, depression, schizophrenia, and alcohol dependence, were constructed as multi-ethnic PGS using both European-ancestry and non-European-ancestry GWAS summary statistics. We selected these five as candidate predictors for our composite PGS because they were the only traits with multi-ancestry GWAS data available, ensuring applicability across the diverse ethnic composition of our ABCD cohort and planned Korean validation sample as it was mentioned above.
From these five candidates, we employed Elastic Net regularization to identify the optimal weighted combination. The target outcome for optimization was a depressive symptom score derived from KSADS-COMP in the pretraining set (n=4,741). We used symptom scores rather than diagnostic status because the pretraining set deliberately excluded all participants with lifetime MDD diagnosis, this exclusion was necessary to maximize the sample size available for the subsequent fine-tuning set where MDD classification was performed. The Elastic Net model was fitted to maximize explained variance (R²) while minimizing the number of independent variables through stepwise elimination across 5,000 resampled train-validation-test splits. This iterative data-driven approach consistently identified three PGS, Depression, BMI, and Alcohol Dependence, as providing optimal predictive performance. PTSD and Schizophrenia PGS did not improve explained variance beyond these three and were therefore excluded. The final composite PGS was computed as: Composite PGS = (β_Depression × GPS_Depression) + (β_BMI × GPS_BMI) + (β_Alcohol × GPS_Alcohol Dependence), where the β coefficients represent the optimal weights from the best-performing Elastic Net model.

1) Clinical Rational for why biological vulnerability markers matter
We respectfully acknowledge that our symptom-based benchmark model outperformed our biological model for 2-year prediction (AUROC 0.767-0.815 vs. 0.61-0.67). However, we emphasize several critical considerations.
First, the critical window for intervention. Once youth develop a clinical first episode of depression, they enter a high-risk trajectory characterized by substantial recurrence (46-50%), treatment resistance (~40%), chronicity (18% remain persistently unwell into adulthood), and elevated comorbidity. Early-onset depression is "particularly malignant and recalcitrant," being "more chronic and debilitating than depression beginning in adulthood." Recent reviews emphasize that "identifying young people at risk for recurring or chronic depression is critical," with optimal identification occurring before first clinical episode onset. [ref needed]
Second, the timing and nature of symptom-based prediction. Our symptom-based model utilized baseline behavioral symptoms (CBCL internalizing problems at ages 9-10) to predict clinical depression diagnosis at ages 11-12. While effective for this near-term prediction, subclinical symptoms in early childhood show mixed results for predicting outcomes in the more distant future. For example, symptoms reported at age 5 had "very limited accuracy" in predicting depression at ages 14 and 21 [1]. This temporal limitation is particularly relevant for preventive interventions, which ideally target individuals years before clinical episode onset.
2) Target Population and Implementation Strategy
We clarify that our model is not intended for universal screening but as a second-tier assessment for asymptomatic youth already identified as high-risk through more accessible first-tier methods.
Tier 1 (accessible screening): Family history of depression, polygenic risk scores, environmental risk factors
Tier 2 (targeted neuroimaging): For high-risk youth without current clinical symptoms (asymptomatic), our model provides enhanced risk stratification (8.5-24% improvement over PGS-only models)
This tiered approach mirrors established medical practice where costly imaging is reserved for genetically vulnerable individuals. We acknowledge that both PGS calculation and neuroimaging involve costs; however, these must be weighed against the substantial lifetime burden of early-onset depression.
3) Current Limitations and Future Directions
We recognize important limitations. Our model predicts from biological vulnerability (genetics + brain) without information about environmental stressors that trigger depression onset. Depression emerges from gene-environment interactions; our model captures only the vulnerability component. Notably, the ABCD dataset lacks detailed assessment of life stressors between baseline and follow-up, precisely when environmental can trigger likely precipitated depression in affected youth.
Despite this limitation, our model retained 80-88% of symptom-based model performance using only biological vulnerability markers. We position this as proof-of-concept that multimodal biological integration enhances prediction beyond unimodal approaches. Future research integrating environmental stressor assessments alongside genetic and neuroimaging data may enable pre-symptomatic prediction approaching symptom-based performance while retaining applicability before first episode onset, the critical window for primary prevention.
